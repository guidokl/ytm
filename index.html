01

Ambient & Drone

6

Brian Eno gave the idea its name in 1978 with Music for Airports: music that can be ignored as easily as it can be listened to. Since then ambient has split into many rooms. Some are warm and slow-pulsing, some cold and beatless, some are built from a single sustained tone that changes so slowly you only notice it in hindsight. Drone takes that last idea to its end point: duration, overtones and the space between the speakers become the actual material. What connects all of it is a shift in attention, away from melody and event, towards texture, atmosphere and time itself.

Stars of the Lid and Kali Malone represent the modern guitar and organ drones, while Lustmord, Eleh and Yellow Swans lead into darker and heavier territory. Long tracks, slow listening.

Experimental & Sound Design

3

Here the sound itself is the composition. The line runs from musique concrète in late-1940s Paris, where recorded noises were cut and looped on tape, through the electronic studios of Cologne and the BBC Radiophonic Workshop, to IDM, glitch and today's sound sculptors. Methods change, from razor blades and tape to granular synthesis and modular systems, but the question stays the same: what happens if a sound is treated as material rather than as a carrier for melody? Rhythm research, texture, digital precision and broken machines all live in this corner.

Stockhausen built electronic music from sine waves in Cologne, Varèse and Xenakis wrote for the Philips Pavilion at the 1958 Brussels World Fair, and Delia Derbyshire and Daphne Oram shaped the sound of the BBC Radiophonic Workshop. Tape loops, sine waves and recorded doors: strange, historic and surprisingly modern.

Dub Techno & Dub

8

Dub began in Jamaica around 1970, when engineers like King Tubby and Lee Perry started remixing reggae B-sides at the mixing desk: vocals out, drum and bass up, everything else thrown into echo and spring reverb. In the early 90s Moritz von Oswald and Mark Ernestus carried that method into Detroit-influenced techno with their Berlin label Basic Channel. The result was dub techno: a chord stab dissolving in delay, a deep bass, a steady kick, and a lot of hiss and space. This family covers both sides: the Jamaican and UK roots of dub and everything that grew out of Basic Channel.

It starts in Berlin around 1993 with Basic Channel and Maurizio, the releases that defined the sound, moves through Rhythm & Sound, where Jamaican singers returned to the music that had borrowed from them, and the more abstract Chain Reaction label in the late 90s. From there it follows the 2000s experiments of Pole and Vladislav Delay and the US revival around Echospace and DeepChord in Detroit. Listen for how little changes from track to track and how much that little matters.

Organic & Roots

3

Electronic music with soil under its fingernails. This family gathers music where machines meet older things: hand percussion, flutes, field recordings, forests and rivers, or the pastoral side of 1970s German electronics. Some of it comes from Jon Hassell's idea of a Fourth World music, some from Andean and African rhythms meeting club culture, some from Kosmische musicians recording in farmhouses in the countryside. The common thread is warmth and a sense of place, music that feels grown rather than programmed.

IDM & Electronica

5

In the early 1990s some producers started making techno for listening rather than dancing. Warp's Artificial Intelligence compilation (1992) gave the movement a home, and the awkward label IDM, short for intelligent dance music, stuck despite everyone disliking it. From there the music split in two directions: melodic and nostalgic, built on worn tape and analogue warmth, or broken and glitchy, built on fractured rhythms and digital errors. Electronica is the wider, looser family around it: songs, folk instruments, voices and samples shaped by the same studio-as-instrument thinking.

    How techno learned to sit still. By the mid 90s the field widened with Aphex Twin's I Care Because You Do, Mouse on Mars, Luke Vibert as Wagon Christ and the first Squarepusher records, which pushed jungle breaks into the mix.

Techno

5

Techno was born in and around Detroit in the 1980s, when Juan Atkins, Derrick May and Kevin Saunderson fused Kraftwerk, funk and the city's futurist imagination into machine music. From there it travelled to Berlin, the UK and everywhere else, splitting into countless forms: stripped-down minimal, melodic and progressive builds, hypnotic and deep, or slow and nocturnal. This family is less about peak-time pressure and more about repetition as a way of listening, where small changes over long stretches carry the whole track.

Later the sound was picked up by Biosphere in Norway and Plastikman in Canada, and later still by producers like Donato Dozzy, Voices From The Lake, Barker and Skee Mask, who keep proving that a kick drum and a wide-open space can coexist.

Basic Channel later fused this sound with Jamaican dub.

Drum & Bass

4

Drum & bass grew out of the UK rave scene in the early 1990s, when producers sped up and cut apart breakbeats and placed them over heavy sub-bass inherited from reggae sound system culture. Jungle came first, rough and rooted in dub and ragga; drum & bass refined it into many directions, from liquid and atmospheric to dark, technical and neurofunk. At its best it holds two tempos at once: frantic drums on top, slow and deep bass underneath.

Goldie's Metalheadz circle answered with a darker, more precise sound, heard here in J Majik, Photek and Source Direct.

The 2010s added polish with Netsky, Etherwood and Lenzman, and the last section shows where the melodic side stands today, closing with Calibre.

The final section looks back at jungle itself: Lee Gamble's Diversions 1994-1996 turns old jungle mixtapes into drumless fragments, while Fracture and Djrum rework breakbeats with a collector's ear.

  4. VC-034

In London, 4hero ran Reinforced Records and moved towards orchestral soul on Two Pages (1998), pointing to the broken-beat scene that followed.
